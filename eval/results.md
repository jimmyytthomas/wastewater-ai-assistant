# Evaluation results

## 1. What is a technology-based effluent limit?

**A good answer should include:** mentions treatment technology performance and effluent guidelines

**Decision:** allow

**Answer:**

The sources describe technology-based effluent limitations (effluent guidelines) as being developed from EPA's assessment of pollution control technologies for an industrial sector: "For each industrial sector, EPA assesses the performance and availability of the best pollution control technologies and pollution prevention practices that are available for an industrial category or subcategory" [7]. These limitations are generated from performance data obtained from "model technologies selected as the basis for the effluent guidelines," and "each facility has the" proper operation of those model technologies tied to the limitations derived from them [3]. The effluent guidelines themselves "are promulgated for various industrial categories in 40 CFR, Chapter I, Subchapter N - Effluent Guidelines and Standards - Parts 400-471" [7][4], and applicability sections "often define certain industrial operations or other criteria (e.g., production or process wastewater flow thresholds) that identify whether a facility is regulated by the effluent guidelines" [6].

The sources do not provide a single, complete formal definition of "technology-based effluent limitation" as a standalone term.

## 2. What are the BOD5 and TSS limits for the Niland wastewater treatment plant?

**A good answer should include:** numeric BOD5 and TSS limits

**Decision:** allow

**Answer:**

The sources state that for the Niland Wastewater Treatment Plant, BOD5 (5-day @ 20 Deg. C) limits are 45 mg/L Average Monthly and 65 mg/L Average Weekly, with corresponding mass limits of 188 lbs/day Average Monthly and 271 lbs/day Average Weekly [8]. TSS limits are 56 mg/L Average Monthly and 84 mg/L Average Weekly, with mass limits of 233 lbs/day Average Monthly and 350 lbs/day Average Weekly [8].

The basis for these limitations is also identified: BOD5 is based on 40 C.F.R. § 133.105(a), and TSS is based on 40 C.F.R. § 133.105(d) and 133.101(f) [7]. Removal Efficiency for BOD5 and TSS is based on 40 C.F.R. § 133.105(a)-(b) [7].

## 3. What turbidity limit applies to filtered wastewater under Title 22?

**A good answer should include:** a turbidity number in NTU

**Decision:** allow

**Answer:**

The sources describe turbidity limits for filtered wastewater under two different filtration provisions in Title 22.

Under §60301.320(a), filtered wastewater that has been coagulated and passed through natural undisturbed soils or a bed of filter media must meet turbidity limits of: (A) an average of 2 NTU within a 24-hour period; (B) 5 NTU more than 5 percent of the time within a 24-hour period; and (C) 10 NTU at any time [1].

Separately, where filtration is provided pursuant to Section 60301.320(a) without coagulation, the filter effluent turbidity must not exceed 2 NTU, the turbidity of the influent to the filters must be continuously measured, the influent turbidity must not exceed 5 NTU for more than 15 minutes and must never exceed 10 NTU, with capability to automatically activate chemical addition or divert the wastewater should the filter influent turbidity exceed 5 NTU for more than 15 minutes [3][4][6][2].

Disinfected tertiary recycled water must also be continuously sampled for turbidity using a continuous turbidity meter and recorder following filtration, with compliance with the daily average operating filter effluent turbidity determined by averaging recorded turbidity taken at four-hour intervals over a 24-hour period [5].

## 4. What did EPA find about PFOA and PFOS in sewage sludge?

**A good answer should include:** risk thresholds may be exceeded in some scenarios

**Decision:** allow

**Answer:**

The sources describe that the EPA's Draft Sewage Sludge Risk Assessment for PFOA and PFOS indicates that in some scenarios, the EPA's acceptable risk thresholds may be exceeded when sewage sludge containing PFOA and PFOS is land applied for beneficial reuse or surface disposed [2]. Traditional wastewater treatment technology does not remove or destroy PFOA or PFOS, and these chemicals typically accumulate in the sewage sludge [3]. PFOA and PFOS have strong chemical bonds, meaning they do not break down on their own in the environment or in our bodies, and the chemicals can move from soils to groundwater or nearby lakes or streams, and be taken up into fish, plants, and livestock [3]. These factors combine to raise questions about the potential risks associated with the presence of PFOA or PFOS in sewage sludge that is land applied as a soil conditioner or fertilizer (on agricultural, forested, and other lands), surface disposed, or incinerated [3].

The draft risk calculations model risk associated with sewage sludge containing 1 ppb PFOA or PFOS, which is on the low end of measured U.S. sewage sludge concentrations, and reflect median exposure conditions (e.g., 50th percentile drinking water intake rates) rather than high end exposure conditions; they also do not take into account non-sewage sludge exposures to PFOA and PFOS, do not account for the combined risk of PFOA and PFOS, and do not account for additional exposures from the transformation of PFOA and PFOS precursors [7]. As a result, risk estimates that account for multiple pathways, multiple sources of exposure, and multiple PFAS would be greater than presented in this draft assessment [5][7].

If the final risk assessment indicates that there are risks above acceptable thresholds when using or disposing of sewage sludge, the EPA expects to propose a regulation under CWA section 405 to manage PFOA and/or PFOS in sewage sludge to protect public health and the environment [8].
